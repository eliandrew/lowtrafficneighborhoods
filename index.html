Neighborhood-wide check

What these checks cover

Local access checks all 47 local street segments for an entry and an exit from a perimeter road. A separate check traces both directions of travel to catch blocked approaches and loops with no way out. This checks the simplified network, not individual driveways, curb access, emergency turning space, or engineering feasibility.

Crossing counts stop when a route first reaches a perimeter road. A route that leaves and re-enters the neighborhood is not counted as a local-only crossing. Adjacent-edge trips and same-edge return trips can still be possible. Timing estimates depend on assumed speeds and signal delays.

01 / The idea

Still connected.
Just a little quieter.

Think of the main roads as hallways and the neighborhood areas as rooms. You can still get into every room. The aim is to stop the rooms becoming shortcuts.

01

Home is still on the way.

The proposal is designed to keep every address reachable by car from a main road. Some trips take a different approach; most intersections stay as they are.

02

A turn, not a dead end.

Right-turn-only changes a driver’s direction. The goal is to keep a way out from every approach; the live checks flag missing exits and loops.

03

Keep the short way on foot.

Gaps in the proposed islands let people walking and biking continue through. The goal is a calmer, more comfortable local street network.

What the street model tells us

Seven can work.
Placement makes the difference.

The original seven keep access open, but they do not form closed traffic cells. An exhaustive search found a stronger seven-intersection arrangement for one specific goal: stopping local-only trips between opposite edges.

Corner-cutting still needs attention.

The repositioned seven still connects some gateways on adjacent edges. The ten-intersection option reduces that count to four. Neither result predicts real traffic volumes or proves a design is ready to build.

More restrictions can make access worse. Right-turn-only at Carr Place & N 39th leaves westbound traffic with no northbound exit in this T-junction. Four right-turn-only corners around one block can also trap vehicles in a loop. Custom mode checks for both.
How the alternatives were selected

The search evaluated all 32,768 subsets of the 15 interior four-way intersections in the supplied map. It rejected configurations with inaccessible segments or any travel direction that could not reach the perimeter. Among the remaining configurations, it minimized opposite-edge connections, then adjacent-edge connections. Seven is the smallest number that eliminates all 64 opposite-edge connections under those rules; two seven-intersection arrangements do so.

Seattle Streets data confirms Carr & N 39th is a T-junction and identifies the short upper Carr–Woodlawn connection as N 38th Ct. It also shows that the original distance scale was too large; the explorer now uses about 0.29 mile east–west and 0.45 mile north–south.

This search does not optimize driveway access, resident travel time, cost, emergency response, or real traffic redistribution. It excludes other interventions and changes to perimeter roads. The repositioned seven is a candidate for investigation, not a construction recommendation.

03 / Let's talk details

Good questions.
Honest answers.

This is a starting point for a neighborhood conversation. Here's what the concept proposes, and what still needs to be worked out.

What happens to today's shortcuts?

Right-turn-only circles interrupt direct cross-neighborhood trips. These are the first forced turns along some familiar shortcuts; the route explorer compares complete trips.

Drivers may find other paths. The model cannot establish whether actual traffic volumes or travel times would change as intended.

Can residents, visitors, and deliveries still get in?

The proposal aims to retain car access to every address from at least one perimeter road: Stone Way, N 40th, N 35th, or Wallingford Avenue. Right turns provide an exit at each treated intersection, rather than creating dead ends.

Getting to a particular home may mean approaching from a different direction. Try the Coming home and Visit a neighbor examples, or choose your own points on the map.

What about parking?

The original concept proposes residential permit parking in all three areas. That would require a separate city process; changing the intersections does not create a permit zone.

Less through-traffic might mean fewer outside drivers searching for parking, but that is an outcome to measure. Re-striping unused corner space might add parking, subject to sight lines, pedestrian safety, and fire access. No additional spaces are assumed.

How would fire trucks and larger vehicles get through?

The concept suggests low, mountable curbs on the medians and central islands so emergency and service vehicles can cross them when necessary. Vehicle turning paths and response access need review with SDOT and the relevant services.

The initial sketch suggests medians roughly a foot wide and 25 feet long, with five-foot gaps at the circle. Those are starting dimensions, not an engineered or accessibility-approved design. Right-turn-only signs would reinforce the physical layout.

Are there still ways to cut through?

Yes. These are planning areas, not fully sealed traffic cells. Keeping changes to seven intersections leaves some indirect paths open:

  • N 37th at Densmore stays open. Drivers can use N 36th, Carr Place, and N 37th to reach Wallingford Avenue.
  • A north–south zigzag remains: Woodlawn → lower N 38th → Carr Place → N 39th → Ashworth. An all-approach right-turn-only device at Carr Place and N 39th would leave an approach without an exit at that T-junction. The laboratory compares other layouts.

A trial should measure these routes as well as any traffic displaced onto the perimeter roads.

How are the routes and travel times calculated?

The explorer uses the original page's simplified street network, with its overall scale checked against Seattle Streets data and the upper Carr–Woodlawn link corrected to N 38th Ct. It finds the lowest estimated driving time between your points, without U-turns. In each configuration, cars may only turn right at the marked intersections. The turn rules are shared by the route planner and the network access checks.

It assumes 20 mph on local streets, 25 mph on perimeter roads, a 25-second delay at the four perimeter corner signals, and smaller delays for turns and other intersections. The scale is approximately 0.29 mile from Stone Way to Wallingford Avenue, calibrated against Seattle Streets centerlines. This also gives about 0.45 mile north–south. Individual block lengths remain schematic.

At an intersection endpoint, the trip starts or ends there; an incoming turn is checked only when passing through. This is a schematic comparison, not live directions. It excludes real-time traffic, exact signal timing, gradients, and trips outside the mapped network. Small estimated differences should not be read as precise predictions.

Is this an approved city plan?

No. This is a community concept for discussion, not an adopted SDOT project. A real proposal would need neighborhood input, engineering and accessibility review, emergency-service coordination, and traffic monitoring.
